# Fact sheet — John Napier

Everything Napier is allowed to state as fact. Sources are listed at the end.
To be checked with the Posner Center before the December exhibit.

## Life
- John Napier, 8th Laird of Merchiston. Born 1550 at Merchiston Castle near Edinburgh; died 4 April 1617.
- Lived at Gartness in Stirlingshire until about 1608, then at Merchiston Castle.
- A landowner, a devout Protestant theologian, and a mathematician by passion.

## Logarithms
- Published *Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio* in 1614 ("Description of the Wonderful Rule of Logarithms").
- The work is commonly said to have taken him around twenty years.
- Logarithms let complex multiplications and divisions be done by far simpler additions and subtractions.
- Henry Briggs, professor of geometry at Gresham College in London, travelled to Edinburgh in 1615 to meet him. According to an account by John Marr, reported later by William Lilly, the two men spent almost a quarter of an hour "each beholding the other with admiration, before one word was spoken."
- Briggs and Napier discussed rescaling the logarithms; Briggs later produced base-10 (common) logarithms.

## Rabdologia (1617)
- Published in 1617, the year of his death. Latin title *Rabdologiae*, meaning roughly "calculation with rods."
- Describes three devices:
  1. **The rods** ("Napier's bones"): square rods, each face carrying the multiples of one digit, written in squares split by a diagonal (tens above, units below). Commonly made of bone, ivory or boxwood.
     - How they are used: to multiply a many-digit number by ONE digit, lay the rods for that number side by side, read the row for the digit, and add along the diagonals from right to left, carrying as needed. Example: 425 × 6 → row 6 reads 2|4, 1|2, 3|0 → 2550.
     - To multiply by a many-digit number, do this once for each of its digits and add the partial results, shifted by place. The rods do not give the product of two many-digit numbers in a single reading.
  2. **The promptuary**: a larger box of strips (MacTutor: 200 ivory strips) for multiplying bigger numbers.
  3. **Local arithmetic**: calculation on a chessboard by doubling — in effect, binary arithmetic.
- He improved Simon Stevin's decimal notation and helped popularise the decimal point (the full stop) to separate whole numbers from fractions; *Rabdologia* uses it.

## The rods after 1617
- Rabdologia also shows how the rods help with division and with square and cube roots.
- Sets were made for decades after his death; surviving sets from later in the 1600s are in boxwood and in brass.
- In 1628 the Jesuit astronomer Giacomo Rho (Chinese name 罗雅谷, Luo Yagu), working in China, wrote *Chou Suan* (筹算, "calculating with rods"), a Chinese adaptation of Rabdologia that explains multiplying, dividing and taking square and cube roots with the rods.

## The promptuary (Promptuarium Multiplicationis)
- Described in an appendix to Rabdologia. *Promptuarium* is Latin for a storehouse.
- Its parts: number strips (each carries the multiples of one digit, like the rods), mask strips (which Napier called excised or perforated strips: one per digit, cut with triangular holes), a board to lay them on, and a box to store them. In Napier's design the top of the box was the board.
- How it is used: lay the number strips for the first number side by side, then lay the mask strips for each digit of the second number across them. Only the digits needed show through the holes; add them along the diagonal bands to read the answer. A worked example often shown is 772 × 396.
- It multiplies a many-digit number by a many-digit number without writing down partial results, though some adding in the head is still needed.
- Napier's example used strips one finger wide and eleven fingers long, enough to multiply two 10-digit numbers into a 20-digit answer.
- It needs far more pieces than a set of rods, and it was never widely used, perhaps because it was harder to make and laying out the strips took nearly as long as pen and paper.
- The only known surviving promptuary is in a small cabinet at the National Archaeological Museum of Spain in Madrid. The cabinet is wood with bone inlays; it holds a set of Napier's rods in its upper part, and its drawers hold the promptuary: 200 flat strips and 100 perforated ones (300 pieces in 30 drawers). It is thought likely to have belonged first to the Academy of Mathematics founded by Philip II of Spain, then the Royal Palace and the National Library, before the museum. The historian Erwin Tomash studied it ("The Madrid Promptuary", 1988).
- In this exhibit the wall shows a plate example: 7 × 98765 = 691,355.

## Location arithmetic (calculation on a chessboard)
- The last part of Rabdologia: "location arithmetic as performed on a chessboard".
- Each letter stands for a number twice the one before: a = 1, b = 2, c = 4, d = 8, e = 16, f = 32, g = 64, and so on. A number is written as the letters that add up to it: 87 = abceg (1 + 2 + 4 + 16 + 64).
- Two of the same letter make one of the next (aa = b, bb = c, …), so numbers can always be tidied into one of each letter.
- On the board, counters stand on squares; moving a counter one square doubles its value. By moving counters he could add, subtract, multiply, divide and take square roots.
- In effect it is binary arithmetic, one of the earliest uses of binary as an aid to calculation: 13 = acd = 1101.
- Napier enjoyed it; in a modern English translation his preface calls it more of a lark than a labour.
- It never caught on and was treated as a curiosity, probably because turning numbers into the letters and back was more trouble than it saved.
- After his time: Gottfried Leibniz published a paper on binary arithmetic, using only the characters 0 and 1, in the Paris Academy of Sciences volume for 1703. Binary later became the basis of electronic computers from the 1940s onward and of almost all computers today.
- In this exhibit's floor game, visitors stand on squares: 9 = da (8 + 1) and 12 = dc (8 + 4); then the wall works out 9 × 12 = 108 = cdfg (4 + 8 + 32 + 64).

## Other work
- 1593: *A Plaine Discovery of the Whole Revelation of Saint John*, a work of theology. In it he reckoned the Last Judgement would fall between 1688 and 1700. (Do not quote or repeat the book's polemics against other faiths.)
- 1596: a list of "secret inventions" for defending the realm: burning mirrors to set enemy ships alight; a piece of artillery; "a round chariot of mettle" to break enemy ranks; and a device for draining water from mines.
- 1598: a patent for manuring land with common salt.

## Legends (present these as stories told about him, not as certain fact)
- Neighbours believed he was a warlock or dabbled in magic.
- The black rooster: to find a thief among his servants, he had each stroke a black rooster in a dark room, saying it would crow for the guilty one. The bird was covered in soot; the servant with clean hands was the one too afraid to touch it.
- The pigeons: he is said to have rid his fields of a neighbour's pigeons by scattering grain soaked in spirits, then gathering the birds when they were too drunk to fly.
